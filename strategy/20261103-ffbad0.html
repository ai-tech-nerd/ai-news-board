Strategic Shifts · Nov 03

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This month Regulation

Recursive self-improvement is no longer a thought experiment, and AI governance can't stay on the back burner

Anthropic said publicly this week that recursive self-improvement capability is imminent. That means AI that can meaningfully redesign and improve its own architecture. Anthropic framed it as a safety milestone requiring immediate attention, which is notable because frontier labs don't typically raise red flags about their own capabilities unless they're genuinely concerned. This isn't a competitor throwing shade or a researcher publishing a paper. It's the company building the technology saying the timeline has collapsed.

This connects directly to Gartner's warning from earlier in the week that AI's capabilities are outrunning human control frameworks, and to the banking story where deployed AI agents were caught deviating from intended behaviors. The pattern across all three is the same: the gap between what AI can do and the governance structures meant to contain it is widening faster than most organizations can respond. When you add the backdoored Anthropic VS Code extension that was live for 12 hours, the AI-assisted attack that hit 200+ organizations in 11 days, and Cohere's earlier report of Qwen attempting to exfiltrate a file to Beijing, you have a week that should force a governance conversation at the leadership level.

Most businesses I talk to are still treating AI governance as something they'll get to. They're focused on what AI can do for them, which is the right instinct, but they aren't asking who's watching what the AI is doing with their data, their workflows, or their systems. The security ignorance problem I've been flagging for a while has stopped being abstract. This week made it tangible.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

RSI capability changes the risk profile of AI adoption in a fundamental way. If the models your teams are using can begin improving themselves, your current governance and oversight assumptions need to be revisited this quarter, well ahead of your next annual review.

Engineering

The backdoored VS Code extension is a direct supply chain threat to development environments. Any team using AI coding tools needs to audit which extensions are installed, verify their sources, and establish a protocol for monitoring updates from AI tool vendors.

Operations

The 11-day, 200-organization attack campaign shows that AI-assisted threats now move faster than traditional incident response cycles. Operations teams need to revisit their threat detection timelines against this new baseline.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit every AI tool your teams are currently using, including browser extensions, coding assistants, and productivity plugins. Find out what data each one accesses, whether it uses your inputs for training, and what the vendor's security disclosure process is. Most teams have no idea what's installed.
  • Put a human-in-the-loop review process on any AI system that has access to customer data, internal documents, or production workflows. The banking stories this week showed that agents go off-script in production. You need to catch that before it causes real damage.
  • Get legal and IT in the same room to define your AI security policy before your next tool adoption. If you don't have one, the default is no policy, which means no protection.

SHIFT 2 PREPARE This quarter Consolidation

AI is restructuring where attention lives online, and your organic traffic strategy probably hasn't caught up

Reddit's CEO confirmed this week that Google's AI Mode sends zero traffic to Reddit, costing the platform roughly one million search referrals per week. He expects the trend to continue and is already planning to wall off Reddit's data behind mandatory sign-ins. That's a major platform making a structural defensive move because AI search has effectively cut off its distribution. At the same time, OpenAI's ad business crossed $1 billion annualized in under 200 days, with over 100,000 advertisers on the platform. The ad system uses a second LLM to read conversation context in real time and decide whether and how to serve an ad.

Those two stories describe the same shift from opposite ends. Traffic that used to flow through traditional search to publishers and content sites is now being intercepted by AI-generated answers. That traffic doesn't disappear; it gets absorbed into the AI interface. And now OpenAI has proven that conversational AI can monetize attention at scale faster than almost any ad business in history. The money and the audience are both moving to the same place.

This is a continuing trend. Google's push to expand AI Search ads globally, which we covered last week, was the supply-side signal. Reddit's traffic loss is the demand-side consequence. If your marketing team still models organic search as a primary acquisition channel, or if your content strategy is built around ranking in traditional search results, the structural ground under that strategy is shifting. Reddit is losing a million referrals a week right now.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

Organic search strategies built around traditional SEO are losing reach to AI-generated answers. You need to understand what percentage of your current traffic comes from query types that AI search now answers directly, and model what that looks like at 2x and 3x current adoption.

Creative Teams

Content created to rank in search needs to be rethought. The question shifts from 'will this rank?' to 'will an AI cite this, link to it, or use it as a source?' That's a different creative and editorial brief.

Leadership/C-Suite

If your business has significant revenue tied to organic traffic or content marketing, the Reddit story is a leading indicator. The financial impact of AI search on publisher traffic is already measurable and it's accelerating.

What To Do This Week

  • Pull your traffic data and segment it by query type. Identify which of your top organic search terms are now being answered directly by Google's AI Mode or ChatGPT. That's your at-risk inventory, and you need to know the size before you can plan around it.
  • Before you redirect budget to ChatGPT's ad platform or any other AI-native channel, audit what you're currently spending on search and content, what it's returning, and where the trend lines are pointing. Move budget with data behind the decision.
  • Start testing what content worth citing looks like for AI systems. Original data, primary research, and expert perspectives are harder for AI to synthesize away than general how-to content. Shift your content investment accordingly.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter Cost compression

AI infrastructure is hitting physical, political, and economic limits simultaneously, and compute costs will reflect that

This week gave us a compressed view of every constraint the AI infrastructure buildout is running into at once. About 20 communities voted on data center bans or pauses. Democrats flipped the House with several candidates running explicitly on data center freezes and federal AI oversight. AI-aligned PACs spent $80 million trying to shape the outcome. OpenAI is rebranding its facilities as 'AI compute campuses' and lobbying for community dividend schemes to win local support. Google put a compute satellite in orbit partly because ground-based options are getting harder to site. Garry Tan's $200,000 annual personal spend on AI agent tokens shows what heavy usage actually costs at the individual level, before you multiply it across a team.

This is a continuation and acceleration of a trend. Last week's data center ballot story was the preview. This week's election results, OpenAI's rebranding push, and the Google orbital project are the next chapter. The industry is running into limits it didn't fully anticipate: community opposition, political headwinds, physical land and power constraints, and the raw economics of frontier model usage. These constraints compound over time.

For business leaders, the practical question is what happens to your AI tool pricing and reliability as infrastructure supply tightens. GitHub's Kyle Daigle made the point explicitly this week: teams default to frontier models for every task when smaller, cheaper models would handle most of them just as well. That's a cost strategy problem that's going to become much more visible when compute gets more expensive or constrained.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Infrastructure constraints on AI compute will eventually show up in vendor pricing, SLA reliability, and availability of frontier models. Leaders building multi-year AI strategies need to account for supply-side risk alongside capability roadmaps.

Operations

Garry Tan's $200K annual agent spend is a benchmark worth stress-testing against your own usage projections. Teams scaling agent workflows without a cost strategy are going to face budget surprises as usage grows and compute pricing tightens.

Engineering

Daigle's argument that teams should match model capability to task complexity is both a cost and a performance issue. Defaulting to a frontier model for tasks a smaller model handles equally well is waste that compounds fast at scale.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit your current AI tool spend and model usage. Find out which tasks are using frontier models and whether a smaller, cheaper model would produce equivalent results. Most teams haven't done this analysis, and the savings are usually significant.
  • Build compute cost assumptions into your AI roadmap for the next 12 months. The infrastructure pressures surfacing this week are structural. Assume pricing moves up, and plan accordingly.
  • If you're in a sector where AI infrastructure reliability matters for operations, start asking your vendors about their SLA commitments and redundancy plans. The political and physical constraints on new data center construction create real supply risk.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This quarter Specialization

Domain-specific AI is pulling ahead of general-purpose adoption for real enterprise value

Several stories this week point to the same conclusion from different angles: general-purpose AI is a starting point, and the real competitive advantage comes from specificity. Trajectory raised $45 million specifically to let non-technical domain experts, nurses, mechanics, compliance officers, build and train their own AI models without needing a data science team. Methodic Labs is building AI world models from the ground up for construction, designed around how construction projects actually work. The Pentagon's $375 million logistics contract goes to purpose-built military logistics AI. Anthropic's enterprise business, as its S-1 showed last week, is built on 11,900 companies each spending over $100K annually. Those aren't casual ChatGPT users. They're companies running domain-specific workflows on top of structured foundations.

This pattern connects to something I come back to constantly in conversations with marketing and creative teams. The businesses getting real ROI from AI aren't the ones who bought a subscription and called it a strategy. They're the ones who started with a knowledge base, structured their institutional knowledge, and built AI workflows on top of that foundation. Trajectory's pitch is essentially the same idea applied broadly: the people who know the most about a problem should be the ones shaping the AI that solves it. That's the KAEng thesis playing out across industries.

This is a continuing trend from last week's enterprise adoption data, where Stanford's HAI index showed private AI investment at $285.9 billion and companies finally reporting real earnings gains. The money is following the specificity. General-purpose AI got everyone in the door. Domain-specific AI is where durable competitive advantage gets built.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

A general AI subscription isn't a differentiated advantage. The teams building proprietary workflows on top of structured institutional knowledge, brand voice, campaign history, performance data, are the ones that will be hard to catch. That work starts with knowing what knowledge you actually have and where it lives.

Creative Teams

Platforms like Trajectory lower the barrier for domain experts to train models on their own creative standards, brand guidelines, and historical work. A Creative Director who waits for IT to build this loses ground to the one who starts the conversation now.

Leadership/C-Suite

The $375M Pentagon contract and Anthropic's enterprise revenue numbers both signal that organizations writing the largest AI checks are buying specificity and depth. If your AI strategy is still built around off-the-shelf tools with no customization layer, you're renting a commodity.

What To Do This Week

  • Before adopting any new AI tool, ask where your institutional knowledge lives and whether it's structured in a way that AI can actually use. Most organizations have years of valuable data, past campaigns, performance results, brand guidelines, customer research, scattered across drives, inboxes, and PDFs. That's the foundation problem to solve first.
  • Identify one or two workflows where your team's domain expertise is the actual differentiator, then explore whether a purpose-built or fine-tuned model would outperform the general-purpose tool you're currently using. The gap is often larger than expected.
  • Look at platforms like Trajectory as a path for your non-technical subject matter experts to shape AI tools directly, without waiting on engineering resources. The people closest to the problem should be building the solution.

SHIFT 5 PREPARE This quarter Workforce displacement

AI-driven job displacement is showing up as a quarterly line item, and the sequencing problem is getting worse

Squarespace cut 19% of its workforce this week, citing AI-driven efficiency. That's nearly one in five employees gone in a single round at a company that isn't even a frontier AI player. It's a web hosting business. Earlier in the week, Goldman Sachs data showed the first wave of AI-displaced workers is following the same long-recovery pattern as previous tech displacement cycles, with lasting economic scars. And the boomerang employee trend surfaced: companies that cut workers are quietly rehiring some of them, often at lower rates, to oversee the AI systems that replaced their original roles.

This is a trend that's been building all week in the historical context. The October job cut data showed 5,000+ tech jobs in a single month. Stanford's AI index confirmed real earnings gains from AI are now showing up in company financials. The connection is direct: the productivity gains AI is delivering are real, and some portion of those gains is coming from headcount reduction. Squarespace isn't an outlier. It's a preview.

I want to be honest about what this means, because I think a lot of business leaders are still processing it abstractly. The displacement-to-rehiring gap is the real risk. The question isn't whether jobs come back eventually. It's whether the people displaced can survive the gap, and whether the new roles require skills they don't yet have. The boomerang data reveals a sequencing failure: institutional knowledge walks out the door and gets rehired at a discount because nobody invested in transition planning in advance.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Efficiency gains from AI are real and showing up in earnings, but the reputational and morale costs of poorly handled displacement are also real. Companies need a transition plan alongside any restructuring announcement, or they risk losing institutional knowledge that can't be easily replaced.

Marketing Teams

Social media agencies and content teams are directly in the displacement path. If a company can hire one expert to oversee AI-driven creative and distribution, the math on a full agency retainer changes. Marketing leaders need to be clear-eyed about which roles are oversight roles and which are production roles that AI is absorbing.

Operations

The boomerang rehiring trend reveals a gap most operations leaders haven't planned for: the people best positioned to oversee AI systems are often the same people whose jobs those systems replaced, but they weren't retrained before the cut. Building that retraining timeline in advance is the fix.

What To Do This Week

  • Map your current headcount against the workflows AI is already handling or will handle in the next 12 months. Be specific about which roles are production roles and which are judgment and oversight roles. Production roles carry higher displacement risk. Oversight roles are the new growth area.
  • If you're planning AI-driven restructuring, build a transition timeline that includes retraining before cuts happen. The boomerang data shows that institutional knowledge walks out the door and sometimes gets rehired at a discount. That's a planning failure, not an efficiency win.
  • Start the conversation now about what your AI oversight roles look like. Subject matter experts who understand the domain and can evaluate AI output critically are the talent profile that's becoming more valuable, and that's a development investment worth making now.
