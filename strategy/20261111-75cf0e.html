How today's AI developments apply to your business.

Executive Summary

The capability gap between frontier AI and what most businesses are actually using just widened again. Anthropic's Fable 5.2 landed at the top of the intelligence leaderboard today, beating a Google model that hasn't even shipped yet, while coming in 20% cheaper and 30% faster than its predecessor. A Wharton professor gave it one open-ended prompt and got back a 90-second video with an original soundtrack and a twist ending. He called it 'almost AGI.' I'd call it a signal that the distance between what these models can do and what most teams are asking them to do is growing faster than most leaders realize. If your team is still using AI mostly for drafting emails and summarizing documents, you're behind by more than a little.

At the same time, today's stories make clear that speed without structure is becoming its own crisis. Notion's CEO said a codebase can become uninterpretable within a week of engineers running their own agents. Claude Code accidentally exposed thousands of API keys and secrets to public GitHub repos. Grok Build leaked confidential data from multiple organizations. These are a pattern: AI is producing work faster than the humans overseeing it can track, understand, or secure. That's the speed trap, and it applies to any team deploying AI agents without clear oversight and audit processes in place.

There's a third thing worth your attention. OpenAI acquired a travel startup, ChatGPT launched business profiles that make it function more like Google for local search, and Meta's Muse Ember cut agent task costs by two-thirds compared to Claude's Haiku. The platform consolidation story I've been watching all week is accelerating. These companies aren't just building models anymore, they're building the places where people and businesses will live. If you haven't thought about what it means for your brand's discoverability when ChatGPT becomes a search destination, that conversation needs to happen before the end of the quarter.

Strategic Shifts · Nov 11

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This quarter cost compression

Frontier models are getting cheaper and faster simultaneously, which breaks the old cost-quality tradeoff

For the past few years, the working assumption was simple: better AI costs more. That's no longer true. Fable 5.2 tops the intelligence leaderboard while costing 20% less and running 30% faster than its predecessor. Meta's Muse Ember matches Claude Haiku 5.5 on the same benchmark at roughly one-third the per-task cost, $0.14 versus $0.41 per agent task. These are simultaneous gains across capability, speed, and price. That combination changes the math on AI adoption in a real way.

This connects directly to what I keep seeing with clients who delayed AI investment because the ROI case felt uncertain. The uncertainty was partly justified when you had to choose between cheap-and-limited or capable-and-expensive. That tradeoff is collapsing. What Anthropic and Meta released today means you can run more capable workflows at lower cost than you could six months ago. If your team benchmarked AI tools in early 2026 and decided the economics didn't work, those numbers are out of date.

This continues a trend building all week. Anthropic's model improvement velocity, which METR measured at 1.9x acceleration since October 2025, is showing up in actual product releases now. The improvements are shipping to production, and the Wharton professor's demo of Fable 5.2 producing a fully realized 90-second video from a single open-ended prompt is the kind of concrete proof point that moves this from benchmark news to business-relevant signal.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Any AI budget decision made more than six months ago needs a fresh look. The cost and capability assumptions that shaped those decisions have shifted materially.

Operations

High-volume agentic workflows that were too expensive to run at scale three months ago may now be economically viable. That changes which process automation projects belong on the roadmap.

Marketing Teams

If cost was the barrier to running AI-assisted campaign generation or audience analysis at scale, it's worth revisiting those use cases with current pricing.

What To Do This Week

  • Pull the actual per-task or per-token costs from whatever AI tools your team is currently using and compare them against today's Fable 5.2 and Muse Ember pricing. The gap may justify switching or supplementing.
  • If your team ran an AI ROI analysis before mid-2026, run it again. The inputs have changed enough that old conclusions may no longer hold.
  • Before adding new tools, audit which AI tools are already in use across your team and what they're actually being used for. You can't measure improvement without a baseline.

SHIFT 2 ACT NOW This month enterprise adoption

AI agents are creating security exposures that most teams don't know they have

Today brought two separate, significant security failures from AI coding tools. Grok Build leaked confidential data from multiple organizations, including information tied to SpaceXSI. Claude Code accidentally exposed thousands of API keys, tokens, and other secrets to public GitHub repositories because developers didn't realize their settings files were being tracked. These are different failure modes: one was the agent itself leaking data, the other was developers not understanding how the tool was handling their files. Both point to the same underlying problem. When teams adopt AI tools quickly without fully understanding how they work or what access they have, sensitive information ends up in places it shouldn't be.

This is the security ignorance problem showing up in a concrete way. Most people don't realize the tools they're using have access to far more than they intend to share. An agentic coding tool that can read a codebase can also read whatever credentials and configuration files live in that codebase. If the people deploying it don't understand that, they're exposed.

The Notion CEO's warning about speed traps ties directly to this. When AI is producing code faster than the humans overseeing it can review it, security hygiene breaks down. Someone commits a settings file without reading it first because the agent already did the heavy lifting and they trusted the output. That's exactly where the exposure happens. This pattern is new this week but it's going to get worse as agent adoption accelerates.

Who This Affects

Engineering

AI coding agents with broad file system access are a credential exposure risk. Teams need to audit what files these tools can read and whether any sensitive config files are being tracked before they commit.

Leadership/C-Suite

If your teams are using AI coding or productivity tools and you haven't asked what data those tools can access and where that data goes, you have an open governance question that needs an answer.

Operations

Any AI agent workflow that touches internal systems, databases, or credentials needs a defined access scope and a human review step before it's trusted to run unsupervised.

What To Do This Week

  • Ask your engineering team today which AI coding tools are in active use, what repository and file system access those tools have, and whether any configuration or settings files from those tools are being committed to source control.
  • Run a search across your public and private repositories for any files associated with AI tool configurations. Claude Code and Grok Build are confirmed vectors today, but this is a category problem, and a vendor-specific audit won't be enough.
  • Establish a written policy for AI tool access scope before your next sprint cycle. What can the agent read? What can it write? Who reviews output before it ships? Human oversight is mandatory when the tool has access to your infrastructure.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter consolidation

ChatGPT is becoming a platform where customers find and transact with businesses, and that changes discoverability

OpenAI acquired Odessia, an AI-native travel startup, today. On the same day, it launched business profiles in ChatGPT that make the product function more like Google for local and business search. These two moves together signal a clear direction. OpenAI is building a platform where people don't just ask questions and get answers, they discover businesses, book services, and complete transactions. That's a fundamentally different product than a chat interface, and it's a direct move into territory Google has owned for decades.

ChatGPT already handles roughly 20% of Google's query volume, a number that surfaced earlier this week. The business profiles launch is the next step: if people are coming to ChatGPT to find information, give businesses a presence there so that traffic has somewhere to land. The Odessia acquisition extends this into end-to-end agentic transactions where the AI books the table, confirms the reservation, and handles the follow-up. Travel is the test case. If it works there, the same model applies to any service business.

For a VP of Marketing, this deserves attention right now. SEO and Google Business profiles have been the foundation of local and service business discoverability for 15 years. If even a portion of that search intent migrates to ChatGPT over the next 12 to 18 months, businesses without a presence there won't show up. We've seen this movie before. The companies that ignored mobile search in 2010 spent years catching up.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

ChatGPT business profiles are live now. If your brand isn't claiming and optimizing its presence there, you're invisible to whatever portion of users already searching for businesses in ChatGPT.

Leadership/C-Suite

The discoverability question is shifting from 'are we on Google' to 'where do our customers actually search now.' That's a strategy conversation worth having before it becomes a catch-up problem.

Sales

If OpenAI's agentic transaction model scales beyond travel, buyers may start completing purchase decisions inside ChatGPT. That changes the funnel and where your conversion points need to live.

What To Do This Week

  • Set up your ChatGPT business profile now while it's new. Early presence in a new search surface has historically mattered more than late optimization.
  • Audit where your customers currently find you: search, social, referral, direct. Then ask honestly whether any of that discovery behavior might shift to AI-native interfaces in the next 12 months and whether you have a presence there.
  • Watch the Odessia integration closely over the next quarter. If OpenAI starts enabling transactional completion inside ChatGPT beyond travel, you'll want to understand what that means for your category before it's mainstream.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This month enterprise adoption

The AI speed trap is real, and it reaches well beyond developer teams

Notion's CEO put a specific timeline on something a lot of teams are starting to feel but haven't named yet: one week. That's how long it takes for a codebase to become uninterpretable when engineers are running their own coding agents. A viral Reddit post this week backed it up, describing a codebase that tripled in size but that nobody on the team could follow anymore. The Databricks story is the flip side of this same coin. The company built an AI agent specifically to give the CEO unfiltered feedback because normal organizational channels were already filtering and softening what leadership actually heard.

The common thread is that AI is moving faster than human oversight can keep up with. In engineering, that means code that works but that no one understands. In organizational management, it means decisions made on information that's been processed and softened too many times to be useful. And in the security incidents with Grok Build and Claude Code, it means exposed credentials because someone trusted agent output without reading it carefully.

I've said for a while that human-in-the-loop is fundamentally a communication problem: the human's job is to set direction clearly, oversee what the AI produces, and correct for what they couldn't articulate well the first time. The speed trap is what happens when teams skip the oversight step because the AI is producing results fast enough that checking feels like it's slowing things down. That's exactly when checking matters most.

Who This Affects

Creative Teams

The same dynamic making codebases unreadable is happening in creative workflows: AI-generated content produced faster than anyone can review it for quality, brand fit, or accuracy. Volume without oversight isn't a win.

Engineering

If your team is running coding agents without defined review checkpoints, you're accumulating technical debt at AI speed. That's a maintenance and security problem that compounds quickly.

Leadership/C-Suite

The Databricks case is worth taking seriously. If AI is producing work faster than your teams can understand it, you need to know that before you discover it through a failure.

What To Do This Week

  • Define explicit human review checkpoints for any AI agent workflow your team is running, before it scales. What gets reviewed, by whom, before it's considered done?
  • Ask your team leads directly: is there any AI-assisted work product shipping faster than you can fully understand it? Most leaders don't ask this question until after something goes wrong.
  • If you're evaluating the Databricks truth-teller agent concept for your own leadership team, the idea is sound. AI as an organizational filter-bypass can work, but only if the knowledge base feeding it is accurate and current, which brings you back to whether your internal information is structured and centralized.

SHIFT 5 MONITOR This quarter consolidation

The frontier hardware supply chain has new governance and geopolitical risk attached to it

Nvidia's stock hit a seven-month low today after Jensen Huang admitted to an affair and the company launched an investigation into possible Chinese espionage tied to that relationship. Corporate governance experts are flagging an accountability gap at the top of the company that supplies the chips most of the AI industry runs on. Nvidia is the critical hardware dependency for the entire AI buildout, and uncertainty about its leadership and security posture is a supply chain risk for every company that depends on AI infrastructure.

This lands in a week when the data center buildout is already under political pressure. Voters backed data center regulation in 40 of 41 ballot measures in the midterms, and Microsoft pulled back from multi-billion dollar data center deals earlier this week. Nvidia governance uncertainty on top of that means the infrastructure layer that everyone assumes is stable looks a bit less stable than it did last week.

For most business leaders, this is a monitor situation. But the Bloomberg projection of TPU sales growing from $12 billion to $135 billion by 2028 is real, and Google's Gen 8 TPU announcement today signals a credible alternative is scaling. The businesses that already understand their infrastructure concentration will be in a much better position than those discovering it in a crisis.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

The AI infrastructure your business depends on runs through a supply chain with active governance and geopolitical risk. It doesn't require immediate action, but it belongs in your risk register.

Operations

If your operations rely on AI services with significant Nvidia hardware dependencies, track this story. Supply disruption or regulatory action on Nvidia could have downstream effects on AI service availability and pricing.

What To Do This Week

  • Map your AI tool stack to understand which services depend on Nvidia infrastructure versus alternatives like Google TPUs or AMD hardware. Concentration risk is easier to manage when you know where it is.
  • Watch the Nvidia board investigation and the Google TPU Gen 8 announcement together. Google's TPU sales projection of $135 billion by 2028 signals a real alternative is scaling, and that matters if Nvidia's situation deteriorates.
  • Keep an eye on the data center regulatory trend from the midterms. If it moves from local ballot measures to federal legislation, it affects everyone's AI infrastructure costs and timelines.
