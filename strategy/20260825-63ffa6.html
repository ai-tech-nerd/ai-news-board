Strategic Shifts · Aug 25

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This month Security and governance

The AI tools already embedded in your enterprise workflows are now confirmed attack surfaces

Both Grok and Microsoft Copilot had active security vulnerabilities disclosed today. Grok can exfiltrate user data when malicious instructions are hidden in encrypted content, a sophisticated prompt injection attack that bypasses standard safety filters. Copilot contained a secret input mechanism that enabled a successful hack. These are production tools, used daily across enterprise workflows, and both flaws represent exactly the kind of attack vector that bad actors actively seek out.

This connects to a pattern I've been tracking across multiple days. AI security vulnerabilities have been surfacing consistently, and most business leaders still treat AI security as an IT concern. The reality is that your marketing team is probably running campaign briefs through Copilot. Your creative team may be using Grok for research or brainstorming. Every piece of competitive strategy, client data, and internal process they feed into those tools is potential exposure. The Instinct AI assistant, also flagged today, is drawing scrutiny for the sheer volume of data access it requires to function at baseline capability. Meta's AI glasses are experiencing explosive demand while detection apps for covert recording prove unreliable.

The security ignorance problem is rarely about malice. Most employees using these tools have no idea their inputs can be exploited, let alone that their data could be exfiltrated through an encrypted instruction they never saw. Most people I work with are also unaware that everything they type into an AI system may be used for training unless they've specifically disabled that setting. Without a clear internal policy covering which tools are approved, what data can be entered, and how to identify suspicious outputs, your organization is operating on trust alone. That is a security posture with no floor.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

The liability question is now real and documented. A breach originating through an AI tool your teams were using without formal policy means leadership made a governance decision by omission, and that decision now has a paper trail.

Marketing Teams

Marketing teams routinely input client data, campaign strategy, and competitive intelligence into AI tools. Without knowing which tools carry active vulnerabilities, they are unwitting participants in a potential data exposure.

Operations

IT and operations need an immediate inventory of every AI tool in active use across departments, prioritizing a security review for tools that handle sensitive workflows or client-facing data.

What To Do This Week

  • Conduct an immediate audit of every AI tool your teams are actively running in their daily workflows, including the ones IT never formally approved. The gap between the sanctioned list and the real list is almost always significant, and that gap is where your exposure lives.
  • Issue a temporary internal guidance document clarifying which categories of data should be withheld from all AI tools until security reviews are complete: client data, financial projections, unreleased product information, competitive strategy.
  • Assign a single person or team responsibility for maintaining an AI tool security log, updated every time a new tool is adopted or a vulnerability is disclosed for an existing one. This does not require a new hire. It requires someone to own it.

SHIFT 2 PREPARE This quarter Cost compression and infrastructure consolidation

The compute cost wave is arriving in 2027, and the preparation window is right now

Nvidia just told Microsoft, Google, Oracle, Amazon, Meta, OpenAI, and Anthropic that AI server prices are going up more than 15% in 2027 on Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell systems. The driver is structural: Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron cannot keep pace with data center demand for DRAM. This is the same agentic AI expansion that has been building as a theme across this newsletter all week.

What makes today's picture more complex is the parallel activity happening across the infrastructure stack. OpenAI built a custom inference chip called Jalapeño to reduce its dependence on Nvidia. Alibaba directed a full $10 billion share sale into custom chip development, a direct response to US export restrictions cutting off access to advanced Nvidia hardware. Apple redesigned its desktop computers for local AI inference, explicitly positioning on-device AI as a cloud-independent alternative. The hyperscalers are all moving in the same direction: control your own compute, reduce inference costs at scale. That is a multi-year, multi-billion-dollar strategy that mid-size businesses cannot replicate. What they can do is make smarter decisions now about which AI workloads genuinely require expensive frontier compute and which ones do not.

Gartner warned last week that agentic AI inference costs will rise fivefold by 2028. Today's Nvidia price warning accelerates that timeline. Businesses that audit their AI spend now, identify which tasks require frontier model capability, and shift appropriate workloads to smaller purpose-built models will absorb the coming cost wave far better than those who keep defaulting to the most powerful and expensive option available.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

AI infrastructure costs are moving from line items to strategic budget decisions. Leaders without visibility into current AI spend have no baseline from which to manage the 15%-plus price increases arriving in 2027.

Operations

Operations teams need a current-state map of every AI API, cloud AI service, and AI-adjacent infrastructure cost in use today, because those costs are about to increase and mitigation strategies need to start before renewals.

Engineering

The case for evaluating open-weight and purpose-built models for specific high-volume tasks is now a cost argument alongside a performance argument. Apple's new hardware makes local inference genuinely viable for certain workloads.

What To Do This Week

  • Map your current AI-related costs across every department: API fees, SaaS tools with AI components, cloud AI services. Most organizations have no consolidated view of this spend, which makes optimization impossible.
  • Identify your three highest-volume AI tasks. For each one, evaluate whether a smaller, purpose-built model could handle it adequately. The NextLM finding today, a specialized small model beating frontier AI on a sales task at a fraction of the cost, previews where enterprise AI economics are heading.
  • Brief your CFO on the Nvidia price increase timeline and its downstream implications for your AI vendor costs. This planning conversation needs to happen before 2027 budgets are locked, giving your team lead time to negotiate or restructure accordingly.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter Specialization over generalization

Specialized models are consistently outperforming general-purpose frontier AI on specific tasks

Harvey built Tenet, its first post-trained legal AI model, on top of an open-weight foundation from Moonshot AI. The notable detail: OpenAI has been an investor in Harvey since 2022. Harvey chose domain-specific performance over brand loyalty to its own investor. That is a public signal from a well-funded, domain-focused AI company that the best model for a specific professional task is the one trained most deeply on the right data, full stop.

NextLM reinforced the same point from a different angle: a specialized small model outperformed frontier AI on a specific sales task at significantly lower cost. These two stories sit inside a broader pattern that has been building across the week. Snowflake introduced model routing to match tasks with appropriately powered models. The Ox Alpha mystery model today demonstrated that anonymous, potentially open-weight models can achieve frontier-level performance on specific benchmarks at zero cost. Paloma, a suspected Qwen release, appeared anonymously in the LMSYS Arena following the same playbook. The direction is clear: frontier models are the right answer for open-ended, high-complexity reasoning. They are expensive overkill for repetitive, well-structured tasks.

For marketing and creative teams, the strategic implication is direct. The question has shifted from 'which one AI platform should we use' to 'which type of model, trained on which domain data, performs best for each specific workflow.' A purpose-built model for brief generation or concept evaluation may deliver sharper outputs than a general assistant, and at a fraction of the API cost. Matching model to task is the efficiency lever most organizations haven't pulled yet.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

High-volume, repetitive marketing tasks like ad copy variations, social captions, and email subject line testing are precisely the work where a purpose-built smaller model could outperform a frontier model at a fraction of the cost.

Creative Teams

Creative directors being asked to justify AI ROI should evaluate whether the tools their team uses are matched to actual tasks. A specialized model for brief generation or concept development may deliver stronger outputs than a general assistant running on expensive frontier compute.

Operations

Operations teams can reduce AI costs meaningfully by auditing which workloads are routed to expensive frontier APIs and identifying candidates for smaller, specialized alternatives with comparable or better task-specific performance.

What To Do This Week

  • List your team's five most frequent AI-assisted tasks and honestly assess which ones require frontier-level reasoning versus which ones follow a predictable, well-defined structure. The structured ones are candidates for specialized or smaller models.
  • Before the next AI tool renewal or purchase decision, ask the vendor directly whether their product uses frontier APIs for all tasks or routes to purpose-built models for specific functions. That answer reveals a great deal about their cost structure and yours.
  • If your organization is building internal AI workflows or automations, introduce the principle of model matching before those systems are locked in. Defaulting to the most powerful model for every task is expensive and, for most defined tasks, unnecessary.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This quarter Workforce displacement

The entry-level workforce disruption is no longer a forecast: Stanford confirmed it today

Stanford published research today confirming that AI's labor market impact is hitting entry-level positions hardest. Goldman Sachs said something similar last week, finding that AI is already slowing job growth in information and communication sectors across major economies. Two separate, credible sources pointing at the same finding in the same week is a signal that leadership conversations need to change.

The compounding problem is what I keep returning to when I read stories like this. Entry-level jobs are the first rung of the career ladder. They are where junior creatives, junior marketers, and junior strategists learn to do the work before they're trusted with more of it. A junior copywriter who spent two years writing product descriptions, even mediocre ones, learned something about craft, audience, and what converts. An academic paper from earlier this week argued that widespread AI adoption could erode the 'cognitive commons,' the collective expertise that gets built and transmitted through those entry-level experiences. If AI handles all of the repetitive, learn-by-doing work, we eventually run short of senior people who learned by doing it first.

For business leaders, the near-term question is the gap between displacement and new role creation. New York lawmakers passed a bill last week requiring businesses with 50 or more employees to report AI-driven job cuts annually. That legislation reflects something real: people are losing entry-level work faster than new AI-adjacent roles are being defined, posted, and filled. The businesses that move intentionally here, defining what the AI-supervised version of an entry-level role looks like before eliminating the original, will be in a stronger position on talent pipeline, employee relations, and regulatory compliance.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

The Stanford finding and incoming NY-style legislation mean workforce AI impact is moving from an HR concern to a governance and legal exposure conversation that belongs at the C-suite level.

Creative Teams

Creative directors who rely on junior staff for execution, resizing, versioning, and production tasks need to deliberately redesign those roles around oversight and quality judgment. Hollowing out that pipeline creates a talent gap that compounds over time.

Operations

HR and operations teams should begin mapping which current entry-level roles are most exposed to AI displacement and proactively designing transition paths, both for ethical reasons and because the regulatory environment is moving in this direction.

What To Do This Week

  • Identify every entry-level role in your organization that currently spends more than 50% of its time on tasks AI can now perform at comparable quality. That inventory is the starting point for redesigning those roles around AI oversight, quality review, and human judgment before displacement creates a skills gap you can't backfill.
  • Define what AI oversight looks like for your most AI-affected entry-level functions. A junior social media coordinator who used to write captions can review AI-generated captions for brand accuracy, tone, and cultural fit. That is a real job with real value, but someone has to define it before it can be hired for.
  • Consult legal counsel on whether your state or jurisdiction is moving toward NY-style AI job cut reporting requirements. Getting ahead of the reporting structure is significantly easier than retrofitting it after cuts have already occurred.

SHIFT 5 PREPARE 12-18 months Enterprise adoption and agentic AI

Agent infrastructure is becoming the real competitive battleground, and most businesses haven't registered it yet

Today's technical product announcements, read individually, look like a routine cycle of developer news. Read together, they describe a coordinated build-out of the infrastructure required to make AI agents actually function at scale. OpenAI acquired the Instant team specifically to build real-time AI agent memory, solving one of the most persistent failure modes in current agentic systems. Keenable is building a web index designed for how agents retrieve information, a fundamentally different architecture from how humans search. Google DeepMind is training agents in an isolated EVE Online server to test long-term memory and multi-agent behavior in persistent, changing environments. OpenAI's head of product said publicly that 'the world seems ready' for widespread agent adoption. Nvidia's AVO agent scored a perfect 100% on the ARC-AGI-3 benchmark with no explicit instructions, completing all 183 levels across 25 public environments.

This connects directly to what Gartner warned last week: agentic AI inference costs will rise fivefold by 2028 as the inference paradox takes hold. The more capable agents become, the more compute they consume per task, and the more tasks they get assigned. What is being built right now, the memory infrastructure, the agent-native indexing, the multi-agent coordination research, is the foundation for a wave of agent deployment that will reach enterprise budgets within 18 to 24 months.

The piece most businesses are completely missing is the knowledge base dependency. Agents are only as useful as the information they can reliably access. An agent operating on scattered, unstructured documents will hallucinate, loop, and fail in ways that are hard to predict and harder to debug. The organizations that have invested in structured, centralized knowledge infrastructure before agents arrive will deploy them far more effectively than those starting from scratch. This is exactly the foundation behind the KAEng concept I've been developing: getting your organizational knowledge into a form that AI can actually use, before the agent wave hits. The preparation window is open, and it will not stay open indefinitely.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

The agent infrastructure buildout happening today is the foundation of the next phase of AI productivity gains. Organizations without structured knowledge infrastructure will be unable to take advantage of it effectively when agents arrive at scale.

Operations

Operations teams should begin assessing the current state of organizational knowledge: where documents live, which versions are authoritative, and how accessible that information is to automated systems. This is the prerequisite work for agent deployment.

Engineering

Engineering teams evaluating agent frameworks should be tracking memory infrastructure developments from OpenAI's Instant acquisition and agent-native retrieval from Keenable, as these will define the capability ceiling of any agent system built in the next 12 months.

What To Do This Week

  • Conduct a knowledge audit: identify where your organization's most important operational documents, processes, and data currently live, who owns them, and whether they exist in a form a structured AI system could reliably query. The gap between what you find and what agents require is your preparation gap.
  • Assign someone to track agent capability milestones quarterly. The OpenAI Instant acquisition and Keenable's Accel-backed build are indicators that the agent deployment wave is 12 to 18 months away, not 3 to 5 years.
  • Before buying any new AI productivity tool in the next two quarters, ask the vendor a direct question: how does this product handle persistent memory and knowledge retrieval? The answer will tell you whether it is built for today's limitations or tomorrow's capabilities.
