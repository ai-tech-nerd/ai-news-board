How today's AI developments apply to your business.

Executive Summary

What happened today with Meta's Muse agent is the clearest preview yet of how AI will restructure commerce, and most businesses aren't positioned for either outcome. Amazon blocked Muse entirely to protect its $56 billion ad business. Shopify partnered with it because agent-driven checkout is a growth driver for a platform built around facilitating transactions. That split isn't a quirk. It's the fault line that's going to run through every industry where AI agents start acting on behalf of customers. The question every marketing and commerce leader needs to answer now is which side of that line their business model sits on. If your revenue depends on humans browsing and clicking, an agent economy is a structural threat. If your revenue comes from completed transactions, agents might be your best distribution channel.

At the same time, the security picture got significantly worse today, and in ways that connect directly to the week's pattern. Muse launched with a confirmed zero-day vulnerability and the kind of deep system access that makes that flaw genuinely dangerous. Google confirmed Gemini was used to breach three companies back in May, five months before anyone disclosed it. Microsoft took down an AI-assisted cybercrime platform that compromised 12,000 accounts. I've been saying for a while that security is the silent risk most businesses aren't taking seriously. This week it stopped being silent. These are documented incidents with real numbers attached, and the disclosure lag on the Gemini breach alone should make every enterprise leader ask hard questions about how quickly their AI vendors surface incidents when something goes wrong.

The third pattern worth naming is one that's been building all week: the economics of AI are shifting faster than most teams have updated their plans. On-device AI is crossing from preference to economic necessity, with one CTO reporting $15,000 to $20,000 per engineer per month in token costs. Anthropic and OpenAI both dropped new models with higher capability and lower prices. Grok 4.7 shipped a meaningful capability jump with no price increase. Snorkel AI tripled its valuation to $3.5 billion on demand for training data. The cost curve is compressing at the model layer while hardware catches up at the device layer. If you haven't looked at your AI spend and infrastructure assumptions in the last 90 days, they're probably already outdated.

Strategic Shifts · Sep 23

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This quarter Enterprise adoption

The agent economy is splitting commerce into two camps, and the division is permanent

Amazon's decision to block Muse and Shopify's decision to partner with it on the same day isn't a coincidence. It's two businesses reading the same signal and arriving at opposite conclusions based on their own economic architecture. Amazon's ad business generates an estimated $56 billion annually, and that revenue depends almost entirely on humans browsing, seeing sponsored listings, and clicking. An AI agent that shops on your behalf skips the entire middle layer. Shopify's model is built around facilitating completed transactions. For them, it doesn't matter whether a human or an agent checks out. The sale still goes through their platform.

This is a preview of a split that's going to land in nearly every sector over the next 12 to 18 months. Businesses whose revenue is attached to the browsing and discovery phase of the customer journey face genuine structural risk from agentic commerce. Businesses whose revenue is attached to the transaction itself may actually benefit. The mistake would be to treat this as a tech story about Meta and Amazon. It's a distribution story about where in the purchase funnel your business captures value, and whether AI agents reaching your customer first is a threat or an advantage.

Meta's stock climbing 20% since launch and Muse hitting number one on the App Store tells you this isn't a niche product. It's moving into mainstream consumer behavior faster than most enterprise teams are tracking. If you sell through platforms that depend on sponsored discovery, it's worth modeling what 20% agent-driven purchase share does to your media spend efficiency, because that number won't stay small.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

If your paid media strategy depends on sponsored listings or browsing-based discovery, agent-driven shopping will erode that channel's efficiency. Start modeling what happens to your customer acquisition costs when agents bypass your ads entirely.

Sales

The consideration phase of the sales cycle shortens or disappears when agents make purchase decisions. Sales teams need to think about how to influence agent decision-making criteria, beyond the assumptions built around human browsing behavior.

Leadership/C-Suite

Your distribution strategy may need a structural review. The question is whether your revenue model survives a world where agents initiate most transactions and the discovery layer your media spend funds gets bypassed entirely.

What To Do This Week

  • Map your current revenue by funnel stage and identify what percentage is tied to human-driven browsing and discovery versus completed transactions. That ratio tells you how exposed you are to agentic commerce.
  • Audit your platform partnerships. If you sell primarily through Amazon or similar ad-dependent marketplaces, start identifying which Shopify-style platforms have incentives to welcome agents and build them into your channel mix now.
  • Brief your media agency or internal team on the Muse-Amazon-Shopify split and ask them to model what 15 to 20 percent agent-driven purchase share does to your current paid channel performance.

SHIFT 2 ACT NOW This month Regulation

AI security incidents are becoming documented, delayed, and systematic, and that combination is the real problem

Today's security picture isn't one story. It's four that connect directly to a pattern that's been building all week. Google confirmed Gemini was used to breach three companies in May 2026, five months before disclosure. Microsoft disrupted an AI-assisted cybercrime platform that had already compromised 12,000 accounts. Muse launched to massive commercial success while carrying a confirmed zero-day vulnerability with deep system access. Last week, a story surfaced about a US government site running a Chinese AI model the FBI had already flagged as malicious. And earlier in the week, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra was classified at the Critical cybersecurity threshold under its own Prepare framework.

The pattern here isn't just that AI tools have security flaws. Every technology does at launch. What's different is the combination of three factors happening at once: the privileged access these agents require to do their jobs, the speed at which they're reaching mainstream adoption, and the disclosure lag. Five months between breach and confirmation is a window during which enterprises were exposed without knowing it and without any ability to respond. That's the part most security teams aren't accounting for when they evaluate AI vendor risk.

I've written about security as the silent risk. This week it stopped being silent. These are concrete incidents with real numbers attached. The AI-assisted cybercrime platform Microsoft took down wasn't a proof of concept. It successfully compromised 12,000 accounts. The Gemini breach wasn't theoretical. Three real companies were hit. If your organization is using frontier AI tools and you don't have a process for tracking vendor security disclosures and incident timelines, you have a gap that needs to close now.

Who This Affects

Operations

The five-month gap between the Gemini breach and its disclosure means your operational risk model needs to account for exposure windows you can't see. Build vendor security disclosure timelines into your AI tool evaluation process.

Leadership/C-Suite

AI vendor security incidents are going to become more frequent and more consequential as agents gain wider system access. The question for the C-suite is whether your current vendor agreements include incident disclosure requirements with specific timelines.

Engineering

The Muse zero-day is a design problem as much as a code problem. Broad system access is what makes agents useful, and it's also what makes a flaw catastrophic. Engineering teams evaluating agent tools need to treat access scope as a primary security variable.

What To Do This Week

  • Pull your contracts with AI vendors and check whether they include mandatory security incident disclosure timelines. If they don't, that's a renegotiation conversation worth having now, before the next breach.
  • Run an audit of every AI tool currently authorized across your organization and document what system access each one holds. Agents in particular. Broad permissions are the variable that turns a flaw into a crisis.
  • Set up a simple internal alert process for AI security disclosures from your key vendors. You shouldn't be finding out about a breach five months later from a press release.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter Cost compression

On-device AI is crossing from a privacy preference to a cost-driven business decision

The clearest cost signal today comes from a CTO who told The Deep View her company is spending 1.5x each engineer's total compensation every month on AI tokens, running $15,000 to $20,000 per engineer per month. That's becoming an unsustainable operating cost for most organizations, and it's changing the calculus around local inference fast. Apple's M5 Ultra Mac Studio delivers 5x the memory bandwidth of comparable Nvidia and AMD boxes, and at $18,299 for the top configuration it can pay for itself inside a single month for a team with heavy token spend. Qualcomm shipped two new smartphone chips with on-device AI as a primary feature. Nvidia's DGX Spark and AMD's Ryzen Halo are both competing in the same local inference space.

This trend has been building across the week. Perplexity's Portable Computer, Hermes, and other always-on local agents keep appearing in the tool stack precisely because cloud costs at scale are becoming a real budget line item. What's new today is the hardware generation to match the need. The M5 Ultra and M6 Mac mini are genuinely competitive with cloud inference for many workloads, and for teams running large open models, the economics now favor buying hardware over paying per token.

For marketing and creative teams, the practical implication is more immediate than it sounds. If your team is using AI tools heavily, and any serious team should be at this point, someone needs to be tracking what you're actually spending. Most teams have no idea what their monthly token costs are across all the tools they're running. That's the audit that should happen before any hardware decision.

Who This Affects

Operations

Token costs at scale are becoming a meaningful operational expense. Teams without visibility into AI spend across departments are likely underestimating total cost of ownership and missing the point where local inference becomes cheaper.

Engineering

The M5 Ultra Mac Studio and M6 Mac mini are now credible infrastructure options for teams running large models locally. The buy-versus-cloud math has changed enough that it's worth running the numbers for your specific workload.

Leadership/C-Suite

AI infrastructure spend needs to show up as a discrete budget line. You can't manage what you can't see, and right now most organizations can't see their total AI spend because it's buried across departmental software budgets.

What To Do This Week

  • Run a simple audit of your current AI tool subscriptions and usage across departments. Add up the monthly token costs, API costs, and SaaS fees. Most leadership teams are surprised by the total when they actually see it.
  • For engineering or creative teams with heavy model usage, have someone model the local inference alternative using Apple M5 or M6 hardware. The break-even calculation is straightforward once you have your current monthly spend.
  • Before your next budget cycle, classify AI infrastructure as its own spend category. It doesn't belong buried in software or cloud budgets if it's growing at this rate.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This quarter Cost compression

AI capability is compressing faster than enterprise adoption can track, and the gap is becoming a cost

Grok 4.7 shipped today with meaningfully better reasoning, longer task endurance, and stronger document output at the same price as Grok 4.6. Anthropic and OpenAI both released new models promising higher capability at lower cost per token. This follows a week that already included Neolab's Jev claiming hundreds of times the speed of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 at a fraction of the cost. The pattern is the same each time: the next generation ships better and cheaper than the last, and the gap between what the current frontier can do and what most enterprise deployments are actually using keeps widening.

This matters for business leaders in a specific way. If your team standardized on a particular model six months ago and hasn't revisited that choice, you're almost certainly leaving capability on the table while paying the same or more than you need to. The model landscape is stable enough to build on, but it moves fast enough that a model review needs to be a recurring item. Teams that treat their AI tooling as a recurring audit item are capturing real advantage, in cost savings and in what they can actually produce.

I keep seeing the same dynamic play out: a team adopts a tool, gets comfortable with it, and stops looking at what else has shipped. That's the same mistake I'd call out in any other part of a technology stack. The audit-before-adopt principle applies here in reverse too: audit what you have before assuming it's still the right choice. The model you're using today may not be the right model for the work you're doing in 90 days, and the price you're paying may not reflect what's currently available.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

If your team standardized on a specific AI writing or campaign tool more than a few months ago, there's a meaningful chance a newer model produces better output at a lower cost. A quick benchmark comparison costs almost nothing and can change your output quality.

Creative Teams

Better reasoning models mean more useful feedback on creative briefs, stronger ideation support, and longer coherent task runs. Grok 4.7's document and presentation capability improvement is directly relevant to teams producing pitch decks and creative proposals.

Leadership/C-Suite

AI model selection shouldn't be a set-it-and-forget-it decision. Build a quarterly model review into your AI governance process, or the competitive teams will be working with tools that are a generation ahead of yours.

What To Do This Week

  • Schedule a quarterly AI model review. Compare your current model choices against what's shipped in the last 90 days on cost per token, capability for your specific use cases, and context window length.
  • Run a benchmark test with Grok 4.7 or the latest Anthropic and OpenAI releases on two or three of your team's most common tasks. The cost to test is minimal; the cost of staying on an inferior model adds up.
  • Document your current AI tooling and who's using what for what purpose. You can't evaluate whether to upgrade if you don't have a baseline of what you're currently running.

SHIFT 5 PREPARE This quarter Regulation

AI governance is becoming a patchwork of competing frameworks, and businesses can't wait for it to resolve

Trump's AI Force announcement today is thin on specifics but loud on direction: no slowdown, AI represents a quarter of the US economy, and safety concerns are a hoax. That puts official US policy in direct conflict with what Anthropic, OpenAI, and others have been saying publicly about the need for coordination and safety standards. Earlier in the week, those same labs were in talks about forming an industry standards body for third-party safety testing. The administration is signaling it has no interest in that kind of restraint.

At the same time, OpenAI says an internal model has solved more than 100 open math problems and has brought in an external advisory group of mathematicians to verify the results before going public. That's actually a responsible disclosure process, and it sits in real tension with the accelerationist posture the administration is pushing. The result is a governance environment where the rules are genuinely unclear and the incentives for different players are pulling in different directions.

For businesses, the practical implication is the same one I've been making for a while: you can't wait for regulation to tell you what's safe to deploy. The regulatory environment won't resolve into a clear framework in any timeframe that helps you make decisions this year. You need your own internal standards for what AI your team can use, what data can go into it, what access it holds, and how incidents get handled. That's basic operational hygiene, and today's news makes clear that no external framework is going to hand it to you.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

There's no coherent federal AI governance framework coming in any useful timeframe. If you don't have internal AI use policies covering data handling, vendor security requirements, and acceptable use, you're exposed in ways that regulation won't protect you from.

Operations

Competing governance signals from the administration and AI labs make procurement decisions harder. Build your own vendor evaluation criteria and stop waiting for industry standards to converge around something actionable.

Marketing Teams

AI-generated content, customer data handling, and agent-driven communications all carry compliance risk that varies by jurisdiction and is changing fast. Document your current AI use in customer-facing work before a regulatory surprise forces the conversation.

What To Do This Week

  • Draft a basic internal AI use policy that covers at minimum: what data can be entered into AI tools, which tools are approved for customer-facing work, and how security incidents from AI vendors get escalated internally.
  • Set your own governance review cadence. Quarterly is reasonable. Treat it as operational risk management and stop tying your AI governance timeline to federal regulation that may never arrive in a usable form.
  • Review your most-used AI tools for data training defaults. Most people don't know their inputs can be used for model training unless they explicitly opt out. That's a security and IP risk that a policy document needs to address directly.
