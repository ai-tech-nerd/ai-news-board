How today's AI developments apply to your business.

Executive Summary

The story I keep coming back to today is the gap between deployment and readiness. Uber rolled out ChatGPT Work to all 30,000 employees in a single move. That's a policy decision, and it's the direction enterprise AI is heading. But that same week, Wharton published data showing only 7% of enterprises actually succeed with AI, and McKinsey found only 6% see meaningful financial gains. Those two numbers don't contradict the Uber rollout. They explain what happens after it. Deployment at scale without a knowledge foundation underneath it, without knowing what your teams need, what data they're working from, and what oversight exists, is how you land in the 93% that never see the return. The infrastructure for always-on, autonomous AI work is now broadly accessible. The question is whether your organization is structured to use it well.

The security picture today is urgent and getting worse fast. GPT-5.6 Sol found PHP vulnerabilities that had sat undetected for a decade. Cloudflare confirmed live attacks before the day was out. Finland, Germany, and the Netherlands pulled government sites offline. CVEs are up 81% in a single quarter, driven by AI accelerating discovery on both sides: researchers finding flaws faster, and attackers turning those flaws into working exploits faster. 1Password had to ship an entirely new authentication product because AI agents are logging into services at scale and the security architecture to contain that didn't exist yet. That's the pattern: agent capabilities are outrunning the guardrails, and most businesses haven't thought through what happens when an AI acting on behalf of an employee makes a bad call with real credentials.

The third thing worth your attention is the AGI timeline. Demis Hassabis shortened his forecast today. Sam Altman published an essay framing three possible endings for the AI safety race and explicitly called the outcome uncertain. These are the people with the most visibility into what these systems can actually do, and they're all moving in the same direction on timing. If your strategic plan treats AI as a tool for incremental workflow improvement, it may be built around assumptions that won't hold by year two.

Strategic Shifts · Oct 23

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This month Security and governance gaps in enterprise AI

AI agents are acting with real credentials, and most businesses have no plan for that

The 1Password launch today isn't a security product in the traditional sense. It's a symptom. AI agents are logging into services on behalf of users at scale, and until today there was no standard infrastructure for doing that without putting plaintext passwords inside the agent's context. 1Password had to build the solution because the problem arrived before anyone had a framework for it. Agent capabilities are outrunning the security architecture built to contain them.

Set that next to today's PHP disclosure. GPT-5.6 Sol found two critical vulnerabilities in software running 70% of the web, flaws that had gone undetected for over a decade. The disclosure was handled responsibly, with two months of coordinated patching before it went public. But Cloudflare confirmed live attacks within hours, and three national governments pulled websites offline. What AI can find, attackers can also find and exploit, often faster. CVEs are up 81% in a single quarter, and security researchers attribute that directly to AI-assisted discovery on both sides.

This pattern has been building all week. An AI agent hacked more than 100 companies for around $5,000 using an open-source framework. OpenAI disclosed four additional rogue AI incidents earlier this week. Chinese state-backed hackers breached ConnectWise and used it as a master key into thousands of downstream businesses. The FTC opened investigations into both OpenAI and Anthropic. Today's stories confirm the direction: agent security is the gap growing faster than any other right now.

Who This Affects

Operations

Any workflow using AI agents to access internal systems, external services, or customer data needs a credential and permissions audit before it goes further. The risk is already active.

Leadership/C-Suite

Enterprise AI deployment without an explicit security framework is a liability decision. The Uber-scale rollout model looks compelling until something goes wrong across 30,000 employees simultaneously.

Engineering

The 1Password model is the right architecture direction: credentials managed at the infrastructure level, never in the agent's context. If your agent stack isn't built that way, it needs to be rearchitected.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit every AI agent your team is running and document exactly what services and credentials each one can access. If you can't answer that question in an hour, that's the problem you need to fix first.
  • Require that any new agent deployment goes through a credential review before launch. No agent should be accessing live systems with user-level credentials unless that access is scoped, logged, and revocable.
  • Check whether your current IT management vendors, including any ConnectWise-adjacent tools, have issued breach notifications or advisories in the last 30 days. The ConnectWise breach exposed thousands of downstream companies through a single vendor entry point.

SHIFT 2 ACT NOW This quarter Enterprise adoption moving from pilot to policy

Enterprise AI is moving from experimentation to blanket deployment, and most organizations aren't structured for what that means

Uber putting all 30,000 employees on ChatGPT Work in a single rollout is the clearest signal yet that the enterprise pilot phase is ending. This isn't a department testing a tool. It's a company-wide infrastructure decision made in one move. And it's happening against a backdrop where Wharton found only 7% of enterprises actually succeed with AI, and McKinsey found only 6% see meaningful financial gains. Those two data points sitting next to each other should make any C-suite uncomfortable.

The reason most deployments fail isn't the technology. It's that organizations adopt AI before they understand what they're trying to fix. They don't have a knowledge foundation for the AI to work with. Teams are scattered across tools, documents have no single source of truth, and the data that would let AI perform well is sitting in silos nobody connected. Claude Duet launching today as a proactive background agent makes this more urgent. An agent that runs tasks while your phone is off and carries memory across sessions needs something coherent to work from. Without a structured knowledge base underneath it, it's an expensive system making confident guesses from incomplete inputs.

I built the first module of a brief-to-launch system specifically because this problem is real. The Creative Brief Builder that AAA is now using works because it starts from structured inputs. Blanket deployment without that foundation is how you get 30,000 employees using AI and still end up in the 93% Wharton says aren't succeeding.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Deployment speed isn't the same as deployment success. If you're pushing AI across the organization without first auditing what your teams actually need and what data they're working from, you're likely to join the 93%.

Marketing Teams

Marketing teams often have the most dispersed knowledge: campaign data, performance analytics, brand guidelines, and customer insights living in different systems that nobody connected. That gap is what limits how useful enterprise AI actually is for your team.

Operations

Company-wide rollouts create immediate governance questions about what employees are asking, what data is being shared, and what the AI is doing with it. That's an operations problem before it's a technology problem.

What To Do This Week

  • Before expanding AI access to your team, run a process audit first. Find out which workflows have the most friction, where information is most scattered, and which decisions are being made without good data. That's where AI deployment will actually pay off.
  • Map your knowledge assets before you scale any AI tool. Which documents are the source of truth for your brand, your campaigns, your customer data? If you can't answer that, your AI will work from whatever it finds first, which is usually the wrong thing.
  • Set a 90-day success metric for any AI rollout before it launches. The Wharton data says most deployments fail to show ROI, and that's partly because nobody defined what success looked like at the start.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter Platform consolidation around agent-first distribution

The platform layer is consolidating around agents, and businesses that miss the window will pay to catch up

Spotify's AI strategy today is worth reading carefully because it's the most honest framing I've seen of what platform survival looks like in an agent-first world. Spotify isn't building a model. It's making sure that when an AI agent decides to play music, it picks Spotify. That means APIs, metadata, and integrations: all the things that make a platform the path of least resistance for an autonomous system making decisions on a user's behalf.

Google is doing the same thing from a different angle. Gemini Personal Intelligence moving into cars is about making Gemini the default intelligence layer across every screen you touch. And OpenAI making its models available on Google Cloud, despite the two companies competing directly at the model level, shows that distribution is beating exclusivity as the dominant enterprise strategy. The model providers need to be everywhere their enterprise customers already are. Claude Duet carries memory and connected apps from existing Claude accounts, making it sticky at the individual level alongside the enterprise level.

This connects to patterns from earlier this week, including the Claude Duet announcement and Databricks' MemCurrent agent memory layer. What's emerging is a clear picture: the companies that win the agent era won't necessarily be the ones with the best models. They'll be the ones agents choose by default because the integration work was done first.

Who This Affects

Marketing Teams

If your brand isn't findable and actionable through the platforms AI agents use to make decisions, you'll lose visibility in a world where agents are doing more of the research and selection work that humans do today.

Leadership/C-Suite

Every SaaS product or service your company sells needs to be evaluated for agent compatibility. Being easy for an AI agent to work with is becoming a product requirement, and it's arriving faster than most roadmaps have accounted for.

Engineering

API quality, metadata structure, and integration surface area are now competitive differentiators. If your product is hard for agents to use, you're invisible in the agent-first stack being built right now.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit your digital properties and services for agent accessibility. Can an AI agent find, understand, and act on what you offer? If your product or service requires a human to navigate it, that's a gap worth addressing in your next planning cycle.
  • Apply the Spotify question to your own category: when an AI agent is making a decision in your space, what would make it choose you over a competitor? Start building toward that answer now.
  • Track the Visa and Mastercard agent payment rails that launched in April. If your business takes payments, understanding how agent-initiated transactions work and what 'agent-flation' could mean for your pricing is worth a conversation with your finance team this quarter.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This quarter AI sector specialization and accountability gaps

AI is moving into healthcare fast, and the accountability structures haven't caught up with the adoption numbers

Two healthcare AI stories today don't belong apart. A $15 billion AI startup funded by advertising is already embedded in physician workflows. Two-thirds of surveyed users have adopted an AI-powered physician search tool. Meanwhile, Microsoft's CEO is publicly claiming their AI outperforms doctors on hard medical cases and describing 'medical superintelligence' as a stated goal.

When two-thirds of users are letting AI guide who they see for medical care, that's well past pilot territory. And when ad revenue funds the tool a doctor uses to treat patients, the conflict of interest is structural. That's the same dynamic that played out with social media and engagement-optimized algorithms, where the business incentive was misaligned with the interests of the people using the product. Meta got hit with a $25 billion fine today for exactly that pattern, which is a useful reminder of how the story tends to end.

This is a continuing trend. Earlier this week OpenAI disclosed four rogue AI incidents involving financial, medical, and government systems. Anthropic is pushing for safety legislation that includes kill switches. The FTC has open investigations into both labs. The regulatory and accountability gap in healthcare AI specifically is getting harder to ignore, and it's moving faster than most healthcare organizations have acknowledged internally.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

If your organization operates in or adjacent to healthcare, the liability and compliance questions around AI tool adoption are more pressing than in most sectors. Waiting for regulation to define your policy is the wrong call.

Operations

Vendor due diligence for AI tools needs to include the business model question. An ad-supported AI tool embedded in a clinical workflow carries a different risk profile than a subscription tool, and that distinction needs to be part of procurement evaluation.

What To Do This Week

  • If your organization uses AI tools in any patient-facing or clinical context, audit those tools' business models and data practices. Who funds the tool, what data does it collect, and who does it share that data with? The Cambridge Analytica pattern starts with ignored fine print.
  • Treat benchmark performance separately from clinical performance. Suleyman's claim about outperforming doctors on hard cases is a benchmark claim. Whether it holds in actual clinical settings is a currently unanswered question, and your procurement process should treat it that way.

SHIFT 5 PREPARE This year AGI timeline compression and strategic planning gaps

The people who built these systems are shortening their AGI timelines, and that gap between their assumptions and yours is widening

Demis Hassabis shortened his AGI timeline forecast today. That matters more than most AGI headlines because Hassabis has historically been one of the most measured, technically grounded voices on this question. He hasn't abandoned caution, but he's clearly revising upward. Set that next to Sam Altman publishing an essay today framing three possible endings for the AI safety race and explicitly calling the outcome genuinely uncertain. That's a notable thing for the CEO of the leading AI lab to say publicly.

Earlier this week, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark stood in front of IMF and World Bank economists and said the singularity has already started. These aren't fringe voices. They're the people who built the systems, and they're all moving in the same direction on timing. Anthropic's IPO documents, showing $16 billion in half-year revenue and compute cost projections stretching to $750 billion, tell you what the capital markets think the timeline looks like too.

I've said for a while that AGI is inevitable but undefined, and the definition problem is still real. Jensen Huang told Lex Fridman 'I think we've achieved AGI' specifically in the context of whether AI could run a billion-dollar company. That's a narrow definition. But the point isn't to settle the definition debate. The point is that the people with the most visibility into these systems are consistently shortening their estimates, and the gap between their working assumptions and most business leaders' working assumptions keeps growing.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

If your three-year strategic plan treats AI as a tool for incremental workflow improvement, you may be planning for a world that doesn't exist by year two. The timeline compression from people closest to the technology warrants a harder look at your assumptions.

Marketing Teams

The roles that feel stable today because they require human judgment are the ones most worth examining. Reasoning and planning capabilities are improving faster than most marketing leaders are tracking, and the gap between perception and reality is usually bigger than expected.

What To Do This Week

  • Run a simple internal exercise: list your team's core functions that depend on human judgment AI can't currently replicate, then check those assumptions against what AI can actually do today. Six months of capability gains can make yesterday's safe assumptions look outdated.
  • If you don't have someone in your organization actively tracking AI capability curves, close that gap. It doesn't have to be a full-time role, but it has to be someone's actual responsibility and show up on their calendar regularly.
