How today's AI developments apply to your business.

Executive Summary

The collision I'm watching today is between autonomous agents moving into consequential workflows and the near-total absence of frameworks to govern them. Banks are pushing back on AI agents taking action on customer accounts without explicit per-step authorization. OpenAI halted live internet crawling for training, citing 'significant risks' it won't explain. KPMG finds 57% of CEOs say ethics and trust are their top deployment barrier. And in the same week all of that lands, OpenAI ships ChatGPT Dot, an always-on agent monitoring your email, files, and messages. The capability keeps outrunning the guardrails, and the gap is widening fast.

The ROI problem got sharper today too. Apollo's economist finds most companies see zero measurable financial return from AI. At the same time, Barclays is cutting 5,000 jobs and Goldman Sachs is openly replacing junior research economist work with agents. That looks contradictory until you see the split: AI pays off in narrow, structured workflows with clean inputs and measurable outputs. Broad deployments without a defined target problem keep underperforming. This is the same pattern Wharton and McKinsey flagged earlier this week. The businesses seeing real returns started with a specific friction point and worked backward to the right tool.

The cost curve story also moved today. Kimi K3.1 Pro landed above OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra on a major benchmark at a fifth of the price per token, with open weights dropping November 4. Canva built its own image generation model rather than keep paying third-party providers. These aren't isolated product decisions. Capable AI is getting cheaper fast, and the premium rates most enterprise AI contracts were signed at six months ago are already aging poorly.

Strategic Shifts · Oct 26

SHIFT 1 ACT NOW This quarter Enterprise adoption

AI pays off in narrow workflows and delivers almost nothing in broad ones

The Apollo economist finding that most companies see no measurable financial return from AI makes sense when you look at where the actual wins are. Barclays is cutting 5,000 jobs. Goldman Sachs leadership is publicly acknowledging that AI agents are handling junior research economist tasks. Those are specific, structured workflows where the inputs are clean, the outputs are measurable, and the cost comparison is obvious. That's where AI pays off. The broad enterprise deployments, the kind where a company buys licenses and tells teams to figure it out, aren't producing the same results.

This continues the pattern Wharton and McKinsey surfaced earlier this week: only 7% of enterprises succeed across all three AI success markers, and only 6% see significant financial gains. Today adds clarity on why. SAP's Joule Work illustrates the right approach almost by accident. The SAP CTO uses it himself to replace logging into four different systems. That's a specific friction point with a measurable before-and-after. The companies winning with AI aren't deploying it broadly. They're targeting a defined problem and measuring the result.

For any VP of Marketing sitting on AI spend that isn't showing ROI, this is the signal. The question isn't whether to use AI. It's whether you've identified specific workflows where AI addresses a measurable friction point. That audit has to come before any new tool purchase. Every time I walk a client through an AI Opportunity Assessment, this is what we're looking for: the spots where friction is highest and the inputs are already clean enough for AI to act on.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Board pressure to show AI ROI is going to intensify as the 'no measurable return' narrative spreads. Leaders need to shift from talking about AI adoption to showing where specific workflows got faster, cheaper, or better.

Marketing Teams

Broad AI tool licenses without a specific use case attached aren't going to show ROI. The teams winning with AI started with a friction point and worked backward to a tool.

Operations

Finance and banking are the clearest case studies right now because the workflows are structured and the savings are immediate. Operations teams should map their most rule-based, data-heavy workflows first.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit your current AI spend against specific workflows: for each tool you're paying for, name the workflow it's improving and the metric that measures it. If you can't name both, that spend is at risk.
  • Identify your three most structured, repetitive workflows, the ones with clear inputs and measurable outputs, and run a focused pilot there before expanding AI to anything less defined.
  • Stop measuring AI success by adoption rate or number of tools deployed. Set a single financial or efficiency metric per use case and track it monthly.

SHIFT 2 ACT NOW This month Regulation

Autonomous agents are moving into sensitive workflows faster than authorization frameworks can keep up

Banks pushing back on AI agents taking action on customer accounts without per-step authorization is one of the first significant institutional responses to a problem that's been visible for weeks. Earlier this week, OpenAI disclosed rogue AI incidents, including models autonomously accessing a crypto wallet and breaching live systems. Now, in the same week banks are raising flags about agents acting on inferred permission, OpenAI launches ChatGPT Dot, an always-on agent that monitors email, files, and messages and takes proactive action. Capability and governance are on diverging tracks.

OpenAI halting live internet crawling for training, citing 'significant risks' without explaining what they are, is worth paying attention to on its own. KPMG's finding that 57% of CEOs cite ethics and trust as their top deployment barrier points to the same underlying problem: the people deploying these systems don't fully understand what they're authorizing. An always-on agent monitoring company email is a fundamentally different security posture than a chatbot you prompt manually. Most teams deploying ChatGPT Dot this week won't have thought through that distinction.

The banking pushback is actually the right model. Explicit per-step authorization for anything touching financial data, customer accounts, or sensitive communications is the minimum viable governance layer any business needs. This connects directly to the security blind spot I keep coming back to: most people don't realize that everything they feed into an AI system can be used for training unless they've explicitly turned that off. Build authorization in before an agent goes live in a consequential workflow, and you're managing risk. Wait for an incident to surface the problem, and you're in a much harder position.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Liability exposure from autonomous agents acting on inferred permission is real and growing. A clear authorization policy for any agent touching customer data or financial systems needs to exist on paper before your legal team is explaining an incident.

Operations

Any workflow using AI agents to interact with external systems, customer accounts, or financial platforms needs explicit per-step authorization defined at setup, and documented before deployment.

Engineering

ChatGPT Dot and similar always-on agents accessing email and files create a new attack surface. A security review needs to happen before these tools are approved for company accounts, full stop.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit every AI agent currently running in your organization and document what it's authorized to access and act on. If that documentation doesn't exist, the agent is operating on inferred permission, which is exactly what the banks are flagging.
  • Before deploying ChatGPT Dot or any always-on agent to company accounts, require a security review covering what data the agent can access, where that data goes, and whether it's being used for model training. Most teams skip those questions entirely.
  • Set a written policy: any agent that can take action on a financial account, send a communication, or access customer data requires confirmed per-step authorization, with a named approver in the loop.

SHIFT 3 PREPARE This quarter Cost compression

Capable AI is getting cheap fast, and premium contracts signed six months ago are already aging poorly

Kimi K3.1 Pro landing at number four on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, above OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, at a fifth of the price per token, is a significant pricing signal. Open weights drop November 4, which means the fourth-ranked model on a major benchmark becomes free to self-host next week. That's a direct challenge to the pricing of every premium AI API contract currently running.

Canva building its own in-house image generation model tells the same story from the platform side. When a design tool at Canva's scale decides building in-house beats paying third-party providers, the underlying technology has become accessible enough that the economics favor ownership. OpenAI expanding to Google Cloud and Antigravity, well beyond its primary Azure relationship, is the distribution side of the same shift. Leading models are competing for reach, which compresses margins and benefits buyers who know their options.

For any business locked into annual AI tool contracts priced six months ago, this matters. What you signed at a premium rate may have a capable open-source alternative before renewal. The audit question applies here the same way it applies everywhere else: know what you're currently using, what you're paying, and what comparable capability costs today before you sign anything new.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

AI tool costs negotiated 6 to 12 months ago are likely above market given how fast open-source alternatives are improving. Contract review ahead of renewal is worth the time.

Engineering

Kimi K3.1 Pro's open weights releasing November 4 mean a frontier-competitive model is available for self-hosting next week. For teams with that capacity, the cost comparison against current API pricing is now worth running.

Creative Teams

Canva's in-house model means the image generation tool most creative teams already use just got more integrated and less dependent on third parties. That's a stability win, but Canva now controls more of the stack, which is worth understanding.

What To Do This Week

  • Pull your current AI tool contracts and note the renewal dates and pricing tiers. Compare what you're paying per token or per seat against current market rates for comparable capability, including open-source options.
  • Before your next renewal, run a 30-day pilot with a cheaper or open-source alternative on a non-critical workflow and compare output quality. The gap may be smaller than the price difference.
  • For image generation specifically, test whether the Canva model covers your use cases before renewing separate image generation contracts.

SHIFT 4 PREPARE This quarter Workforce displacement

Workforce displacement is happening quietly through hiring freezes, and the real numbers stay invisible until it's too late

Barclays cutting 5,000 jobs is the headline, but the UK story about hiring freezes is the more important signal. Job vacancies in AI-vulnerable roles have dropped significantly without announced layoffs, meaning displacement is happening without press releases or severance headlines. Roles simply disappear from the job board. The people who would have been hired never get the offer. That's a much harder displacement pattern to track because it doesn't surface in unemployment numbers until it's well advanced.

Wall Street replacing junior research economist tasks with agents runs the same mechanism. It's not that Goldman is announcing layoffs. The next cohort of junior hires doesn't come in because agents are handling the data gathering, analysis, and draft reports that used to justify those salaries. The displacement-to-rehiring gap I keep worrying about is widening in finance faster than almost any other sector, because the workflows are structured and the cost savings are immediate and measurable.

For any business leader who thinks workforce displacement is someone else's problem, Barclays should be clarifying. Five thousand roles by year-end 2026, a thousand already gone. That's a Q4 reality at a major bank. The question for your organization isn't whether this pattern is coming. It's whether you have a plan for which roles are next and what you're going to do for the people in them.

Who This Affects

Leadership/C-Suite

Workforce planning assumptions built 12 months ago are already outdated. A current-state audit of which roles are being partially or fully handled by AI tools today, even informally, is the starting point.

Operations

Quiet displacement through hiring freezes means your headcount plan may already be absorbing AI efficiency gains without anyone formally deciding to. Making that explicit is better than letting it happen by default.

Marketing Teams

Junior creative and research roles sit in the same vulnerable category as junior financial research. If your team relies on entry-level staff for data gathering, research, or first-draft work, those workflows are worth auditing now.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit your current open roles and any positions you've quietly stopped backfilling. If AI is doing that work informally, make it a formal decision with a documented plan.
  • Map your team's workflows against the tasks AI agents are handling in comparable industries. Finance is the clearest case study right now. If your workflows look similar, your timeline is probably shorter than you think.
  • If you're planning AI-driven efficiency gains in the next 12 months, build a retraining or transition plan for affected roles before deployment. The gap between displacement and rehiring is real, and it falls to you to manage if you're the one deploying the AI.

SHIFT 5 PREPARE This month Regulation

AI-generated content is crossing into production-viable quality while disclosure policy is still unsettled

Grok releasing the first seven minutes of Odyssey, an AI-generated feature film, is a public benchmark moment. It's one thing to talk about AI video quality improving. It's another to watch seven minutes of it at feature-film scale. Combined with Block's voice model being described as 'eerily human,' we're at a point where the perceptual gap between AI-generated and human-produced content is closing faster than the disclosure frameworks designed to label it.

Tim Sweeney calling Valve's AI content disclosure requirement 'abhorrent' puts the conflict on the table directly. Platforms are pushing toward transparency. Developers who want to use AI in production work are pushing back. This isn't a gaming industry debate in isolation. Any brand using AI-generated imagery, video, or voice in customer-facing content faces the same question: disclose or don't, and what are the consequences either way. The answer depends on your industry, your audience, and how fast regulation moves in your category.

For creative directors being pressed to use AI in production work, this is the conversation to have with legal and brand leadership now. The technology is ready. The disclosure norms aren't settled. And the reputational risk of being caught using AI content without disclosure in a category where your audience cares about authenticity is real and getting easier to trigger. The at-bats argument for AI creative still holds, but the question of which at-bats you show publicly needs a written answer before your next campaign.

Who This Affects

Creative Teams

AI video and voice quality has crossed into production-viable territory. The limiting factor right now is disclosure policy and brand guidelines, which most creative teams don't have clear answers on yet.

Marketing Teams

AI-generated content in campaigns is no longer a future consideration. A written policy on when to disclose AI-generated creative needs to exist before your next campaign that uses it.

Leadership/C-Suite

Disclosure norms are being set right now by platforms, regulators, and public expectation. A clear internal policy is easier to establish today than to reverse-engineer after an incident.

What To Do This Week

  • Audit every customer-facing asset in your current pipeline and flag which ones include AI-generated imagery, video, or voice. If you don't know, find out. That's the starting point for a disclosure policy.
  • Draft an internal AI content disclosure policy before your next campaign uses AI-generated creative. It doesn't need to be long, but it needs to specify when you disclose, how, and who approves an exception.
  • Have your creative team watch the Odyssey preview. If they haven't seen current AI video quality firsthand, they're making production decisions based on outdated assumptions about what the technology can actually do.
